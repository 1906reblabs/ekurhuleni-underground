The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 35

The Tembisa Appliance Circuit

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Tembisa Appliance Circuit is not a story about broken fridges. It is a story about a second manufacturing sector — one that takes a machine the formal economy has written off and returns it, priced and warrantied in every way but name, to a household that was never going to buy it new.

The deep story: how a national e-waste stream measured in hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year is intercepted, township by township, before most of it reaches a landfill or a formal recycler — diverted into a layered economy of collectors, repairers, and resellers who return refrigerators, stoves, and washing machines to working order and sell them into a market formal retail has priced out of reach. Along Tembisa's Andrew Mapheto Drive corridor, this economy has built a genuine second-life manufacturing capability — one a national academic survey has, for the first time, tried to measure.

Three supporting micro-stories: the collectors of Esangweni who make daily rounds gathering broken appliances before a single unit reaches a skip; the repair workshops of Umthambeka, where technicians trained on the job diagnose faults formal service centres will not touch; and the resellers of Xubeni, Tembisa's oldest section, who move refurbished units through layby arrangements that function as the trade's own credit system.

The overlooked place: the Andrew Mapheto Drive corridor — Tembisa's main artery, known locally as the Rabasotho Line — where the trade's collection, repair, and resale layers sit within a few streets of each other without ever being named as a single cluster.

The interesting statistic: South Africa generates an estimated 360,000 tonnes of electronic waste a year — roughly 6.2 kilograms per person — of which only about 12% is formally recycled. The rest moves through channels like Tembisa's, uncounted until a 2026 survey of more than eight hundred informal repairers began measuring them for the first time.

Weekly Issue — 18 September 2026

The Second-Hand Circuit

How Tembisa's informal appliance repairers and resellers built a genuine second-life manufacturing economy for fridges, stoves, and televisions that formal retail and municipal waste systems were never designed to handle — and why fixing things, at scale, is a trade the census has only just begun to count.

1. Editorial
The Fridge That Came Back

On Andrew Mapheto Drive in Tembisa — the road planners call the M18 and everyone who uses it calls the Rabasotho Line — a man is loading a refrigerator onto a borrowed bakkie. The fridge left a household in Kempton Park eleven days ago, condemned for a compressor that had started clicking instead of humming. It arrives now at a yard two streets off the main road, where a technician will spend forty minutes with a multimeter and a capacitor from a drawer of capacitors, and where, by Friday, it will be running again — colder, if anything, than it was the week it broke.

Nobody sent this fridge here. No municipal route brought it, no formal recycling contract claimed it, no retailer's take-back scheme logged it. It travelled through relationships — a collector who knows which streets discard which appliances on which days, a technician who learned refrigeration from an uncle rather than a college, a reseller three sections away who will sell it on layby to a family that was never going to afford one new. None of this is written down anywhere a municipal economic profile would think to look.

I have been circling Tembisa's appliance economy for some weeks, drawn by a statistic a research team published this year that nobody in the trade needed to be told: only a small fraction of South Africa's electronic waste — by the researchers' account, roughly one tonne in eight — is formally recycled. The remainder does not vanish. In Tembisa, and townships like it, a meaningful part of it goes to a collector's trolley, a repair bench, and a resale stall, in a sequence so routine the people running it have never thought to call it an industry.

What that research also did, almost incidentally, was put a number on something this publication has described in different registers before but never named directly: a second-life manufacturing sector, hiding inside what the formal economy still calls waste. This issue follows one township's version of it — and asks what it means that the machines the formal system writes off keep working, on average, for years after someone else decides they haven't.

2. The Long Essay
The Manufacturing Trade Nobody Certifies
Section I — The Observation

Walk Andrew Mapheto Drive between the Esangweni taxi rank and Xubeni on a weekday morning, and the appliance trade announces itself in stages. Nearest the rank, handcarts and modified trolleys move between houses and a collection point behind a fenced yard — nothing branded, nothing signed, just a gate that opens when a load arrives. A few streets further, the sound changes: the hum of a compressor under test, the scrape of a technician stripping a wiring loom for parts. Further still, toward Xubeni, the trade becomes visible in finished form — rows of refrigerators and stoves outside a fenced plot, price cards taped to their doors, buyers negotiating the terms on which they will pay a unit off rather than its cost. Three streets. Three stages of the same object's second life.

Section II — The Hidden System

The appliance economy of Tembisa operates across three layers, each performing a function the formal retail and waste-management sectors have, in practice, stepped back from.

The first is collection. Every township generates a daily stream of appliances that have failed, been replaced, or judged not worth the call-out fee a formal technician would charge to look at. Collectors — alone or in twos, on foot or with a borrowed vehicle — have built routes through specific streets and, in some cases, relationships with the damaged-stock channels of nearby retailers, where a fridge with a dented door or a washing machine returned within its cooling-off period is written off far below what repairing and reselling it would cost. A national survey published this year — the first of its kind, covering more than eight hundred informal repairers across eleven South African cities and towns — found repair activity recorded in nearly every case surveyed, with dismantling and refurbishment built on top of it.

The second layer is repair itself, where the trade's technical depth sits. The same survey found its practitioners trained almost entirely outside any formal qualification system, through apprenticeship and intuition developed from several hundred failed compressors. Their customers are overwhelmingly households rather than businesses — people for whom a formal service centre's minimum call-out charge already exceeds what a working used appliance costs outright.

The third layer is resale, where the trade becomes, in the strict sense, retail. Refurbished units move through fixed stalls and yards, priced against new-appliance retail but sold, in a large share of cases, on layby — a deposit followed by instalments the reseller tracks by hand, the same credit architecture this publication has traced in the tombstone and furniture trades elsewhere on the East Rand.

The retailer sees a write-off. The repairer sees eighteen months of working life nobody has priced yet.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What Tembisa's appliance economy reveals is a measurable version of a pattern this publication keeps finding: the formal system's definition of a write-off is an economic judgment, not a technical one. A retailer discards a returned appliance not because it cannot be fixed, but because fixing it costs more, inside a formal wage structure, than the item is worth on a new-goods shelf. Nothing about the compressor changes when it crosses that threshold. What changes is who is willing to spend forty minutes with a multimeter for the margin on the other side of it.

This matters for how Ekurhuleni understands its own waste stream, because the national accounting of e-waste — tonnes generated, tonnes formally recycled — treats everything else as loss: material on a trajectory toward landfill. The Andrew Mapheto Drive corridor is evidence that a meaningful share of that supposed loss is not lost at all. It has changed hands, changed ownership category, and kept working — for years, in many cases — inside a market segment formal retail was never built to serve at a price it could sustain.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The appliance economy strengthens on both sides of the transaction whenever the formal system tightens. On the demand side, currency depreciation and import-cost inflation raise the shelf price of new appliances faster than township incomes rise to meet them, and the customer base for a refurbished fridge or stove grows larger still. On the supply side, the same regulatory framework meant to make South Africa's e-waste stream cleaner has widened the collectors' channel: extended producer responsibility rules in effect since 2021 make retailers financially responsible for the formal disposal of products they sell, so a damaged unit a collector will take off their hands at no cost, with no compliance paperwork attached, has become more attractive to offload informally than before the rule existed.

Neither pressure was designed with Tembisa's repair yards in mind. Both have, in practice, fed them — currency stress pulling customers in from one direction, compliance cost pushing stock in from the other. A trade built on other people's write-offs does not need the formal system to fail. It only needs the formal system's own arithmetic to keep pointing, as it reliably does, toward the door marked exit.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Collectors
Esangweni, Tembisa

Each weekday morning, collectors fan out from the Esangweni taxi rank on foot and with borrowed trolleys, working streets they know well enough to tell, without asking, which households replace appliances on a landlord's cycle and which retailers write off returned stock rather than process a formal claim. They pay nothing for what they collect; their return comes three streets later, at the repair bench. Their knowledge is the route itself — which door, which skip, which morning. It has never once run dry.

2. The Bench
Umthambeka, Tembisa

In a fenced yard in Umthambeka, technicians who learned refrigeration and motor repair from relatives rather than any trade college work through a daily intake of failed compressors, seized motors, and washing machines whose drive belts have worn through. None holds a formal qualification; each can diagnose a fault by sound before a multimeter confirms it. Their price undercuts a formal call-out fee alone, and their turnaround is days rather than the weeks an authorised repairer quotes for a backordered part. What they sell is availability a formal warranty has already given up on.

3. The Layby Yard
Xubeni, Tembisa

Along the older streets of Xubeni, Tembisa's first section, refurbished appliances stand in ranked rows outside fenced plots, each carrying a hand-written price and, for most buyers, a payment plan rather than a single transaction. The reseller tracks every account in a notebook: a deposit on collection, a fixed instalment due on payday, the unit withheld until the balance clears. No credit bureau is consulted; no interest is charged beyond the margin already built into the price. The default rate is low, for the reason it is low in every layby arrangement this publication has traced on the East Rand — a community small enough that a missed payment is never really private.

4. The Map of the Week
The Andrew Mapheto Drive Corridor, Tembisa
25°59′S  28°13′E

Roughly three kilometres. That is the stretch of Andrew Mapheto Drive — the M18, known locally as the Rabasotho Line — running from the Esangweni taxi rank toward Xubeni, Tembisa's oldest section, along which the appliance trade's three layers sit within easy walking distance of one another. In that corridor: collectors' yards identifiable only by a gate and the trolleys parked against the fence; repair benches announced by the hum of a compressor under test rather than any signage; and, closer to Xubeni, the ranked rows of refurbished fridges and stoves that mark the trade's retail end.

What you will not find is a single sign describing any of it as a trade. The road appears, on every municipal map, as a transport route. To walk it on a weekday morning, tracking one appliance from a collector's trolley to a repair bench to a price card three streets on, is to watch Ekurhuleni's e-waste statistics prove themselves wrong.

5. The East Rand Number
88% Estimated share of South Africa's e-waste that never reaches a formal recycler — the balance moving through repair, refurbishment, and resale networks like Tembisa's own

South Africa generates an estimated 360,000 tonnes of electronic waste annually — around 6.2 kilograms for every person — of which the E-Waste Association of South Africa puts formal recycling at roughly 12%. The remaining 88% does not simply disappear. Some is landfilled without record. A meaningful share, by the account of a 2026 survey of more than eight hundred informal repairers across eleven South African cities and towns, is intercepted, repaired, and sold onward — through yards like the ones strung along Andrew Mapheto Drive every week.

6. Closing Reflection
What the Motor Still Has Left

The fridge that left Kempton Park with a clicking compressor will, by the time this issue publishes, be standing outside a fenced plot in Xubeni with a price card taped to its door, most of the way through a second life with no sign of having had a first one. Nobody who buys it will ask where it came from. Nobody who gave it away eleven days ago will ever know where it went. Between those two households sits a trolley, a multimeter, and a notebook tracking a deposit and three instalments — the manufacturing capacity of a trade Ekurhuleni's economic profile has never once listed as one.
