The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 34

The Kempton Park Licence Corridor

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Kempton Park Licence Corridor is not a story about learning to drive. It is a story about a piece of plastic that has become the precondition for almost every entry-level job the East Rand's logistics economy offers — and about the shadow economy of coaches, route-readers, and booking runners that has grown around a testing system too backlogged and too easily gamed to be trusted on its own.

The deep story: how the K53 driving test — a forty-year-old CSIR design out of step with the licences employers now demand — has produced, around every Driving Licence Testing Centre in Ekurhuleni, an informal facilitation economy: coaches who train on a station's specific yard and route rather than driving in general, runners who navigate a booking system a corruption probe found riddled with fraud, and knowledge that has become as important to getting licensed as the driving itself.

Three supporting micro-stories: the yard coaches of Vosloorus, who replicate a testing station's parking bay and incline layout on open ground to the metre; the booking runners outside Germiston's DLTC, who navigate a slot system a 2022 investigation found compromised at its own department; and the route readers of Benoni, who sell knowledge of a station's known road circuit rather than driving instruction in the conventional sense.

The overlooked place: the testing centre precinct at Monument Road in Kempton Park — a facility serving the OR Tambo logistics corridor's job seekers, where the formal queue and the informal coaching economy operate metres apart.

The interesting statistic: South Africa's newly computerised learner's licence test, introduced to close loopholes that made the old paper system so exploitable, returned a pass rate of just 17% in its first full year — a failure rate steep enough on its own to explain why a coaching economy has grown around the gate to a driving licence.

Weekly Issue — 11 September 2026

The Licence to Work

How a shadow economy of yard coaches, booking runners, and route readers has become the last gate between East Rand jobseekers and the driving licences that unlock delivery, logistics, and forklift work — and why passing the K53 test has quietly become a trade of its own.

1. Editorial
Every Test Has a Shape

By half past seven on a weekday morning, the yard at the Kempton Park testing centre already has an audience it did not officially invite. Along the perimeter fence, men and women stand with their hands through the wire, watching a learner reverse into a parking bay marked out in paint decades old. They are not family. Most are instructors, and the applicant failing to align her mirrors with the cone on her third attempt trained with one of them the previous afternoon, on open ground in Vosloorus laid out, cone for cone, to match the bay she is failing to enter.

I have been circling the licence economy of the East Rand for some months, drawn by a detail that seemed almost too mundane to justify an issue: that a driving licence, in a region built on freight and logistics, is not simply a document. It is the precondition for almost every entry-level job that pays above a spaza counter — delivery driving, forklift operation, the Code 10 and Code 14 categories the warehouses and fabricators this publication has already covered require as a baseline. What I had not expected was how thoroughly a shadow instruction economy has grown to close the gap between wanting the document and actually holding it.

The gap is not small. South Africa's K53 test — a defensive-driving system the CSIR designed in the early 1980s and has barely revised since — has long struggled to clear a 40% national pass rate. In 2025, a computerised version meant to close the old test's loopholes returned a pass rate of seventeen percent. Gauteng's own testing centres have faced similar scrutiny: a 2022 provincial investigation found runners, booking fraud, and officials moonlighting as driving-school owners inside the department's own facilities.

Into that combination — a hard test, a compromised booking system, and a job market that will not hire without the card — an informal economy has moved. Not corruption exactly, though corruption sits inside it. Mostly coaching: people who have worked out, through repetition, what a specific station's yard and road demand, and who sell that knowledge for a few hundred rand a session. This issue follows that economy from the practice yard to the DLTC gate.

2. The Long Essay
The Second Curriculum
Section I — The Observation

Walk the fence line of any Ekurhuleni testing centre on a weekday morning and two economies operate within arm's reach of each other. Inside the yard: the official test, run by a uniformed examiner with a clipboard, following a marking sheet unchanged in its essentials since the K53's design in the early 1980s. Outside the fence: instructors in unofficial bakkies, coaching last-minute technique through the wire, adjusting a client's mirror angle with a hand gesture because touching the vehicle would disqualify the attempt. Most have watched this specific yard's test run hundreds of times. They know which cone the examiners actually watch, and that the incline start on this slope catches nine learners in ten who trained on a gentler gradient elsewhere. The official test is standardised on paper. At any given station, in practice, it has settled into a pattern — and a trade has formed around reading it.

Section II — The Hidden System

The K53 facilitation economy of the East Rand operates in three layers, each responding to a different point of friction between an applicant and the licence a logistics-driven regional economy insists they hold.

The first is yard replication. On open ground in Vosloorus and similar patches across the region's older townships, informal instructors have built practice layouts that mimic, cone for cone, the parking bays and incline starts of specific nearby stations. This is not generic driving instruction. A learner booked at Kempton Park trains on a Kempton Park-pattern yard; one booked at Boksburg trains on a different one. The instructor's core asset is not a teaching qualification — most hold none — but the accumulated knowledge of one station's physical layout, refined every time a client reports back on where they lost marks.

The second layer is booking access. Since 2022, when a provincial investigation into Gauteng's Driving Licence Testing Centres found fraudulent online bookings, fee-dumping, and officials with undisclosed interests in driving schools operating inside the department's own facilities, the online slot system has remained a genuine obstacle for applicants navigating it without help. Into that friction, runners have moved: people who understand the booking platform's quirks, who queue physically before dawn when a centre's allocation opens, and who charge a modest fee to secure a slot an applicant working shifts has no realistic way to catch. Not all of this is illegitimate. Much of it is simply persistence, sold as a service.

The third layer is route intelligence for the road test. Examiners at a given station tend, informally but consistently, to run candidates along a limited set of known routes — the same left turns, the same three-way stops, the same awkward merge that has failed generations of local learners. Specialists who have mapped these routes, sometimes by riding along with cooperative former candidates, sell that knowledge as a premium add-on to standard instruction. They are not teaching driving. They are teaching the test.

A driving test is supposed to measure whether someone can drive. At most East Rand stations, it has quietly become a test of whether someone was coached for that specific yard.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What the K53 facilitation economy reveals about Ekurhuleni is a pattern this publication has traced across water, credit, health transport, and now driver licensing: a formal gatekeeping system, fair on its face, that rewards applicants who can afford insider knowledge over applicants who simply meet the underlying competence standard. The K53 test does not discriminate by design. But its opacity — a station's yard, incline, and road route developing a de facto pattern the department neither publishes nor standardises — creates exactly the information asymmetry a coaching economy exists to close.

This matters for how the region thinks about its own logistics labour market. Ekurhuleni's freight, warehousing, and delivery sector — the OR Tambo cargo corridor, the industrial fringe this publication has mapped from Alrode to Wadeville — runs on Code 8, Code 10, and Code 14 licences the way a manufacturing economy runs on trained artisans. A jobseeker with the underlying driving competence but no access to a station's route intelligence faces a genuine, avoidable barrier — not because they cannot drive, but because they were tested against an unpublished local pattern rather than a published national standard. The economy that closes that gap is, in this narrow sense, doing work the testing system should already be doing.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The K53 facilitation economy strengthens, rather than merely persists, at exactly the moments the formal system tightens around it. When the 2022 Gauteng investigation forced a crackdown on the runners and officials it identified, it did not eliminate demand for booking assistance — it raised the value of runners who could show they were separate from the fraud being prosecuted, sharpening the line between a legitimate persistence-for-hire service and an illegitimate one. When the computerised test produced a seventeen percent pass rate, the reform meant to close old loopholes instead widened the market for the specific, repetition-tested coaching that yard replicators and route readers already sold, because a harder, less gameable test rewards genuine preparation more, not less. Each tightening of the formal system — a corruption crackdown, a harder test, a longer backlog — raises the price a well-prepared applicant will pay for someone who already knows what a specific station will ask. The reform and the shadow economy it was meant to discipline have, so far, grown up together.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Yard Coaches
Vosloorus Residential Fringe

On open ground at Vosloorus's edge, informal instructors have built practice yards that replicate, bay for bay, the parking and incline layouts of nearby testing stations. None holds a teaching diploma. Their training is repetition: watching, over years, where clients lose marks at a given station, then adjusting the layout by the metre until the failure stops recurring. A session costs a few hundred rand and runs an hour. Clients arrive with a booked test date; the coach's first question is always which station.

2. The Slot Runners
Germiston DLTC Precinct

Outside Germiston's testing centre, a handful of runners have built a modest business navigating a booking system a 2022 investigation found riddled with fraudulent slot allocations and official collusion. Not all runners work that side of the line. Several simply queue before dawn when a centre's daily allocation opens, or track cancellation windows a shift worker has no chance of catching themselves, charging a flat fee for the wait rather than the outcome. Their clients need the slot for a scheduled job interview.

3. The Route Readers
Benoni Testing Centre

Around Benoni's testing station, a smaller number of specialists sell a narrower service: knowledge of the road-test circuit examiners tend, informally but consistently, to run candidates along. They have mapped the route's known hazards — a merge lane two learners in five fail, a three-way stop with a blind approach — sometimes by riding along with cooperative former candidates. They do not teach driving in general. They teach one station's test, turn by turn.

4. The Map of the Week
The Kempton Park Testing Centre Precinct
26°6′S  28°14′E

A single fence line. That is what separates the two economies at the Kempton Park testing centre on Monument Road — a facility serving much of the OR Tambo corridor's job-seeking population. Inside: the official yard and road test, examiners with clipboards, a marking sheet unrevised since the 1980s. Along the fence and in the parking area beyond it: instructors coaching last-second technique through the wire, runners confirming slot availability by phone, applicants reviewing a coach's notes once more before their names are called.

What you will not find is any signage acknowledging that the second economy exists, or that a fair share of applicants walking through the gate were trained, the previous afternoon, on ground built to replicate what waits on the other side of the fence. To stand at this corner on a weekday morning is to understand that the licence, and the shadow curriculum required to get one, are now inseparable.

5. The East Rand Number
17% Pass rate of South Africa's new computerised learner's licence test in its first full year — steep enough to make coaching for a specific station's pattern as valuable as learning to drive

South Africa's newly computerised learner's licence test, introduced to close loopholes that made the old system easy to game, returned a pass rate of just 17% in its first full year — against a national pass rate that had long struggled to clear 40% under the old paper system. Gauteng's own testing centres remain under the scrutiny that produced a 2022 corruption investigation into booking fraud and official collusion. Numbers this steep price in the value of knowing, in advance, exactly what a specific station will ask.

6. Closing Reflection
The Gate That Moved

The coach on the open ground in Vosloorus does not think of himself as running a shadow curriculum. He thinks of himself as helping people pass a test that, in his experience, asks something slightly different at every station than the manual describes. He is not wrong, and that is the problem the department has never solved: a test meant to certify one national standard of competence has developed, station by station, local patterns real enough to build a business around. The question the licence economy leaves behind is not whether the coaching helps. It is why passing was ever supposed to depend on knowing the difference.
