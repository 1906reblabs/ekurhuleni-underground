The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 41

The Ekurhuleni Title-Deed Gap

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Ekurhuleni Title-Deed Gap is not a story about paperwork. It is a story about two registers — the state's record of who owns a house and the street's record of who lives in it — and about what is documented, and what is not, in the space between them.

The deep story: a 2024 analysis by the Operation Vulindlela titling team put Ekurhuleni's title-deed backlog at an estimated 95,992 properties: 65,255 residential plots on townships whose registers were never opened, and 30,737 subsidy properties on opened registers but still owned solely by state entities. The City says it holds thousands of deeds nobody has collected, and a national tracker reports transfers running at about 25,000 a year across South Africa.

Three supporting micro-stories: the housing liaison desks at the City's customer care centres, which verify an applicant and hand over deeds the City already holds; the door-to-door handovers in Palm Ridge, where about 2,000 deeds were still to be handed out in 2023; and the chair-to-chair transfer, documented in Cape Town but not counted in Ekurhuleni.

The overlooked place: Palm Ridge, south of Katlehong, where four general plans appear in the 2024 analysis, one of them with 4,121 of 5,550 residential properties missing from the deeds data.

The interesting statistic: Ekurhuleni's 95,992 is the largest of the three metro backlogs the analysis estimated, ahead of Tshwane's 91,626 and Johannesburg's 60,642.

Weekly Issue — 30 October 2026

The Deed in the Drawer

Why tens of thousands of Ekurhuleni's subsidy homes have no registered owner — the deeds the City holds, the door-to-door handovers, and the informal route around an incomplete register, from published sources only.

1. Editorial
The Owner of Record

Before a family can hold a title deed, a sequence of public records has to exist. A township must be planned and surveyed, and the Surveyor-General must approve its general plan. The Registrar of Deeds must then open a township register. Only after that can a subsidy house be transferred from the state to an identified beneficiary, who is handed the deed. The Operation Vulindlela titling team, in a June 2024 presentation, called the first half township establishment and the second beneficiary administration, and counted the backlog at each.

For Ekurhuleni it counted 65,255 residential plots, between 50 and 500 square metres with a building on them, in townships with an approved general plan but no entry in the deeds data. It counted a further 30,737 subsidy properties that are in the deeds data but owned solely by state entities. Together that is 95,992, against 60,642 in Johannesburg and 91,626 in Tshwane. These are estimates built from survey, satellite-footprint and May 2024 deeds data, not a house-by-house count.

The City holds some of the paper itself. In February 2022 its Human Settlements department said 10,468 title deeds were gathering dust in its offices. In February 2024 a City statement put unclaimed deeds at more than 22,300. In April 2026 the executive mayor said the City still held 13,177, mainly because of family disputes and untraceable beneficiaries. The figures come from separate statements and may not count the same things.

What no source found measures is how many of these homes now have an occupant who is not the person the state chose. City statements in 2024 referred to a discrepancy between current occupants and rightful owners, and the 2024 analysis lists an informal sale among the cases that need extra steps. This issue follows those two clues from published sources only, as Issue 40 did. Where it infers, it says so, and it says where the documentation stops.

2. The Long Essay
Two Registers
Section I — The Observation

What the published record shows about Palm Ridge is a pattern in a table. The 2024 analysis lists four Palm Ridge general plans. Three are among the fifteen Ekurhuleni townships with the most subsidy properties owned solely by state entities: plan 4975/2016, with all 767 of its subsidy properties state-owned; plan 4385/2002, with 580 of 2,496 (23 percent); and plan 4034/2017, with all 422. A fourth, plan 1193/2010, is among the fifteen with the most residential properties missing from the deeds data: 4,121 of 5,550, or 74 percent. The four sum to 5,890 properties, about 6 percent of the metro estimate. The sum is this publication's; the tables list only the top fifteen in each category, so other Palm Ridge plans may exist.

On 31 March 2023 the City and the Gauteng Department of Human Settlements handed over more than 300 deeds in Palm Ridge. A government news agency reported that about 2,000 were still to be handed out in the Palm Ridge extensions, and that 9,000 deeds held by the City's Human Settlements Department were expected to reach their owners through door-to-door visits by youth brigades. One recipient said his family had lived in the house for 23 years.

Section II — The Hidden System

The gap works through three layers, each documented by a different kind of source.

The first is the township register. Until the Registrar of Deeds opens one, no individual deed can be registered in the township, however long the houses have stood. The titling team's process map lists the steps before that point, from land clearance and town planning approval to Surveyor-General approval of the general plan, and says they are likely to take years to resolve. The August 2026 Operation Vulindlela progress report names legislative challenges to township establishment and proclamation, and to beneficiary administration, as holding up deeds for subsidised housing.

The second layer is beneficiary administration. The rightful owner must be identified and confirmed, with extra steps if a beneficiary has died or an informal sale has occurred, and arbitration if there is a dispute. After registration the deed reaches the household through the City: each customer care centre has a housing liaison officer who checks an applicant's details and hands the deed over. The Gauteng Department of Human Settlements told Parliament that Ekurhuleni had 7,361 deeds handed over and 9,501 registrations completed between 2019 and March 2026.

The third layer is the informal transfer. The titling team compared a R220,000 sale. The formal route listed a conveyancing fee of R10,390, deeds office fees of R642, a rates clearance certificate at R600 and R300 for postage and petties: R11,932, or 5.4 percent of the price. Its timing was uncertain, possibly more than a year with complications, and its recourse was the courts, for registered owners. The informal route listed a street committee fee of about R300, immediate occupation on payment, and social sanction as recourse. A 2013 submission to Parliament described a similar practice in Cape Town, called chair-to-chair, using a police affidavit or a civic-organisation letter, and said such a document had no legal standing in a dispute. No source found measures this practice in Ekurhuleni.

The register keeps who the state gave a house to. The street keeps who lives in it.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What follows is partly this publication's reading. The two registers answer different questions. The deeds register records which person the state transferred a property to, and when. The street's arrangements record who is living in the house now and what they paid. The titling team draws the same line in its own terms: a title deed gives citizens, the city and the financial sector visibility, while informal or community-administered mechanisms can give households security of tenure but can undermine the city's governance role and limit the financial sector's involvement.

The municipal consequence is documented for Gauteng as a whole. The analysis estimated that 59,347 properties on opened registers are likely to generate rates invoices that cannot be delivered, because the invoice is in the city's own name, and that 188,913 plots cannot be billed because they do not exist administratively while being physically occupied and serviced. For Ekurhuleni, valuing each of the 95,992 properties at the analysis's R220,000 rule gives about R21 billion. The arithmetic is this publication's.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The evidence supports a narrower claim than antifragility. The named stress is the cost and delay of the formal route. The informal route does not wait for it: on the titling team's comparison it is cheaper and immediate. That is consistent with informal transfers persisting where formal ones are slow. It is not shown to grow under stress, and no source counts informal transfers over time.

What the sources do show is a loop. An informal sale adds steps to the formal process, because the owner of record must still be reconciled with the person in the house. Law and policy sit on top: the City says a beneficiary may not sell within eight years and that the deed is endorsed for buy-back, and in December 2020 it said it would evict 43 families at Chief Luthuli Park whom it alleged had bought state houses through a criminal syndicate while allocation was under way. This publication reads such positions as discouraging buyers from coming forward. The titling team proposes clarifying regularisation so that an informal purchase with evidence of sale and no dispute is simply transferred, with no evictions and no penalties.

The pace figures are slow. The Business Leadership South Africa tracker for the third quarter of 2026 reported 76,210 transfers nationally over three years, 25,403 a year, and 40 years at that rate to clear 1,000,856 deeds. In Ekurhuleni the City reported distributing 1,220 deeds since 2024, with 800 more planned, while still holding 13,177. If that is two years of work, about 600 a year, clearing 13,177 would take about 22 years. That is an illustration, not a forecast. The August 2026 progress report scheduled township-establishment and beneficiary-verification pilots in Ekurhuleni and Johannesburg for October 2026; the tracker said little is known of their scope, targets or funding. No source found confirms they have started.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Liaison Desk
EKURHULENI CUSTOMER CARE CENTRES

Each City customer care centre has a housing liaison officer who checks an applicant's details and, once satisfied, hands over the deed the City is holding. Applicants bring identification and, per a 2026 notice, proof of residence. The desk sits where the paper does: the City reported 10,468 unclaimed deeds in February 2022, more than 22,300 in February 2024 and 13,177 in April 2026, giving family disputes and untraceable beneficiaries as the main reasons. The sources give no processing times.

2. The Door-to-Door Brigade
PALM RIDGE EXTENSIONS, SOUTH OF KATLEHONG

In March 2023 the City and the provincial department handed over more than 300 deeds in Palm Ridge. A government news agency reported about 2,000 still to hand out in its extensions, and 9,000 held by the City to go out through door-to-door visits by youth brigades. An undated City notice reported close to 4,000 distributed under a Title Deeds Friday programme. The October 2026 pilot named for Ekurhuleni uses a door-to-door method, per the tracker, which found little published on its scope or funding.

3. The Chair-to-Chair Transfer
NO EKURHULENI SOURCE — DU NOON AND MAKHAZA, CAPE TOWN

Documented elsewhere, not counted here. A 2006 study cited to Parliament in 2013 found 36 percent of RDP houses in Du Noon informally transferred, and the submission said such houses sold at about R30,000 against R80,000 formally. A 2024 case study of Makhaza, Khayelitsha, counted 732 of 5,510 properties as sold or occupied informally. The titling team's fee for the equivalent step, a street committee, was about R300. Whether Ekurhuleni's townships resemble these is not established.

4. The Map of the Week
The Palm Ridge Extensions, South of Katlehong
26°26′S  28°9′E

About ten kilometres. That is the distance, computed by this publication, from the reference point Wikipedia lists for Katlehong (26°20′S 28°9′E) south to the GPS point a construction-project listing gives for Palm Ridge Extension 37 (26°25′36″S 28°9′7.5″E). Wikipedia describes Katlehong as extending south to Palm Ridge, and lists Palm Ridge among Alberton's suburbs. The published record for that southern edge shows: four Palm Ridge general plans in the 2024 backlog analysis; extensions where the City reported about 2,000 deeds still to hand over in 2023; and a handover day in March 2023 that reached more than 300 households.

What no source describes is where within Palm Ridge the properties without registered owners lie, or how many have changed hands informally. The coordinates above are Extension 37's alone; Palm Ridge is made of many extensions and no single point stands for it.

5. The East Rand Number
95,992 Estimated Ekurhuleni properties with no registered individual owner in a 2024 analysis — 65,255 on townships with no opened register, 30,737 held by state entities

The Operation Vulindlela titling team's June 2024 analysis estimated Ekurhuleni's title-deed backlog at 95,992 properties. Johannesburg's estimate was 60,642 and Tshwane's 91,626. The Gauteng Department of Human Settlements reported 9,501 registrations completed in Ekurhuleni from 2019 to March 2026, about a tenth of the 2024 estimate; periods and definitions differ, so the comparison is a prompt, not a rate. The figure counts properties, not households, and no source found updates it.

6. Closing Reflection
The Paper and the House

A deed can sit in a drawer at a customer care centre while the house it describes is lived in, inherited or sold on the street. The published record offers three clocks: 25,403 transfers a year nationally, 1,220 deeds distributed by the City over roughly two years, and an informal route that completes on payment. The question the figures leave open is not whether the register will catch up, but what it will find when it does: which names, which families, which sales. Nobody has yet counted that.

— Ekurhuleni Underground, 30 October 2026
