## THEME ANALYSIS — ISSUE 36

The theme of the Vosloorus Crèche Economy is not a story about babysitting. It is a story about who actually runs early education in a township context — and about the tens of thousands of women who have built it, room by room, in the near-total absence of state infrastructure designed to reach them.

**The deep story:** how South Africa's national count of roughly 42,000 early childhood development programmes conceals a much larger operational reality inside Ekurhuleni's townships — home-based crèches running from converted bedrooms, backyard extensions, and repurposed garages, financed almost entirely by parent fees, and reaching a share of the region's under-fives the formal, subsidised system was never built to serve.

**Three supporting micro-stories:** the backyard principals of Vosloorus, who convert a spare room into a classroom for twenty or thirty children and quietly absorb whatever the month's fees don't cover; the bulk buyers of Katlehong, who pool orders across unregistered crèches to bring mealie meal and samp down to a price no single operator could reach alone; and the bronze-certificate runners of Thokoza, who have turned a paperwork process meant to formalise the sector into a modest trade of its own.

**The overlooked place:** the residential streets radiating from Barry Marais Road in Vosloorus, where a converted room behind roughly every third or fourth gate operates, on any weekday morning, as somebody's classroom.

**The interesting statistic:** the 2021 national ECD Census counted approximately 42,000 early learning programmes across South Africa — fewer than half fully or conditionally registered with the state. The rest, concentrated overwhelmingly in townships like Vosloorus, exist in a category no department has ever agreed to call formal.

### 1. Editorial

**The Gate That Opens at Six**

By six in the morning on a residential street off Barry Marais Road in Vosloorus, a gate that looks like every other gate on the block swings open for the day's first arrival: a father in overalls, a child on his hip, already running behind a shift that starts at seven. He hands the child through to a woman who has been awake since five, who has already swept the yard, warmed the porridge, and laid mats out in what used to be her dining room. No sign announces what happens behind that gate. By half past seven, twenty-six children will be inside, and the woman running the room — she calls herself the principal, because that is what the parents call her — will have begun a school day the state has no record of her running.

I have been circling Ekurhuleni's home-based crèche economy for some weeks, drawn less by its existence, which nobody who has lived in a township would call surprising, than by the gap between how much the region depends on it and how little anyone has described how it works. Almost every working parent in Vosloorus, Katlehong, or Thokoza without a grandmother nearby relies on a room like this one. There are, by the most recent national count, somewhere in the region of forty-two thousand of these operations across the country. Fewer than half are registered in any form the state formally recognises.

That is not because the women running them have not tried. It is because the registration process — zoning approval, a building plan, fire compliance, a health inspection, an occupational certificate — was designed for a purpose-built facility and applied, with only modest adjustment, to a converted bedroom in a four-room council house. The subsidy registration is meant to unlock, seventeen rand a child a day until this year's budget raised it to twenty-four, reaches only a fraction even of the centres that already qualify.

Into that gap an economy has grown that this publication has not yet described, though it runs beneath nearly every other economy it has covered: the households keeping several hundred thousand small children fed, safe, and accounted for while their parents run the rest of it. This issue is an account of how that economy works.

### 2. The Long Essay

**The School the Census Missed**

**Section I — The Observation**

Walk the residential grid around Barry Marais Road on a weekday morning and the crèche economy declares itself in details rather than signage: a fence built a little higher than a neighbour's, to keep small children off the road; a hand-painted number taped inside a front window, the day's attendance chalked beside it; a line of small shoes at a doorway left ajar since before six. Nothing on the street distinguishes a converted front room running as a classroom for twenty-six children from the house next door. The absence of distinguishing features is not an oversight. It is the condition the sector operates under — invisible by necessity, because visibility to the wrong department can mean an inspection the room was never built to pass, and a closure the neighbourhood was never offered a replacement for.

**Section II — The Hidden System**

The crèche economy of Vosloorus and the townships around it operates across three layers, each responding to a different piece of a need the formal ECD system reaches only partially.

The first layer is the room itself — the home-based practitioner who has converted a bedroom, garage, or backyard extension into a functioning early learning space, and who is, in nearly every case this publication traced, a woman running the operation as both principal and sole employee, sometimes with a paid assistant once enrolment justifies it. She sets fees at whatever the street can bear — commonly a few hundred rand a month, below the national average the 2021 census recorded, because a Vosloorus household's disposable income after rent, transport, and food leaves little room above that. When a parent cannot pay in a given month, most practitioners describe simply absorbing the loss, because an empty chair costs the neighbourhood more than the missed fee costs her.

The second layer is bulk provisioning. Feeding twenty or more children a meal on a few hundred rand a month per child is not arithmetic that works at retail prices, so bulk buyers have emerged specifically to serve the sector: women who consolidate orders across a dozen or more unregistered crèches in a section, buy mealie meal, samp, and soup stock at volumes no single operator could justify alone, and redistribute it at a modest margin. No supply contract governs the relationship — a WhatsApp group and a shared bakkie do — but it keeps the sector's nutrition costs within reach of what parents can actually pay.

The third layer is registration facilitation. The Department of Social Development's mass registration drive, known locally by its Bana Pele branding, has produced a genuine attempt to formalise the sector — and, alongside it, a small trade in people who understand the paperwork well enough to walk an operator through it: which documents a specific office accepts, which inspector is due in which section and when, how to sequence an application so a fire clearance does not lapse before a health inspection is finally scheduled. They hold no official standing. Most, this publication found, are former practitioners who learned the process the hard way and now sell that knowledge onward.

> *The state built a register for classrooms. It has never learned to count a converted bedroom.*

**Section III — The Broader Insight**

What the crèche economy reveals about Ekurhuleni is a pattern this publication keeps finding in different registers: a formal system built around an assumption of scale and permanence — a purpose-built facility, a fixed address, a stable enrolment — applied without adjustment to an informal system built around flexibility and proximity instead. The requirements are not unreasonable in the abstract; fire safety and sanitation genuinely matter where small children spend their days. What they assume is an institution the township crèche economy was never going to produce, because one that met every requirement would also charge fees no Vosloorus household could pay.

This matters for how Ekurhuleni understands its labour market, because the childcare capacity that keeps parents at work is not a background convenience. It is infrastructure, in the sense this publication has used the word about water, credit, and transport — a system whose failure would be felt everywhere, in a way its continued function is never separately noticed or counted.

**Section IV — The Antifragile Angle**

The home-based crèche economy has expanded through every recent pressure on formal alternatives rather than merely surviving them. When registration backlogs lengthen — as they have, by the sector's own account, across the drive's rollout — the informal room down the street remains the only option a working parent has. When household budgets tighten under inflation, parents who might once have paid a registered centre's higher fees move to the cheaper alternative next door, and the informal operator's enrolment grows precisely when the formal sector's would contract. And when a formal centre closes — a failed inspection, an unaffordable lease, a subsidy that arrived months late — its children do not disappear. They redistribute, within days, into whichever nearby rooms still have space, absorbed by a system with no waiting list and no admissions process beyond a parent at the gate.

### 3. The Hidden Economy

**Three Operations Nobody Is Watching**

**1. The Backyard Principals**
VOSLOORUS RESIDENTIAL STREETS, BARRY MARAIS ROAD CORRIDOR

Along the streets running off Barry Marais Road, women who describe themselves simply as principals run early learning programmes from converted bedrooms and backyard extensions, each holding fifteen to thirty children on fees of a few hundred rand a month. None holds full departmental registration; most have applied and are still waiting. They set curriculum from memory and borrowed workbooks, cook the day's hot meal themselves, and absorb the fees unemployed parents cannot cover rather than turn a four-year-old away. Their qualification, in most cases, is eleven or more years of not having closed.

**2. The Bulk Buyers**
KATLEHONG WHOLESALE FRINGE

Operating from a rented room near Katlehong's cash-and-carry strip, a small number of women consolidate weekly food orders from a dozen or more unregistered crèches across the surrounding streets, buying mealie meal, samp, and soup powder at volumes no single operator could justify and splitting the saving between their own margin and the crèches' shrinking budgets. No supply contract governs the arrangement; a shared bakkie and a WhatsApp group do. Their expertise is nutritional arithmetic — how many kilograms of samp twenty children actually need to get through a month on a few hundred rand a head.

**3. The Bronze Runners**
THOKOZA / SOCIAL DEVELOPMENT REGISTRATION PRECINCT

Around Thokoza's social development offices, a handful of facilitators — mostly former practitioners who registered their own crèches the hard way — now walk newer operators through the Bana Pele registration drive for a modest fee: which documents a specific office accepts, which inspector to expect and when, how to sequence a fire clearance so it has not lapsed by the time a health inspection is scheduled. They hold no official standing. Their value is procedural memory of a bureaucracy that changes its requirements more often than it publishes them.

### 4. The Map of the Week

**The Barry Marais Road Residential Grid, Vosloorus**

*26°21′S  28°11′E*

A few hundred metres of ordinary residential street. That is the footprint of the crèche economy's densest concentration in Vosloorus — the grid of houses running off Barry Marais Road, in a township established in 1963 and granted full municipal status two decades later, where a converted room behind roughly every third or fourth gate operates, on any weekday, as somebody's classroom. In that grid you will find hand-painted attendance numbers taped inside front windows, small shoes lined at doorways left ajar since before six, and, at pickup time, a queue of parents at gates carrying no sign of what is inside.

What you will not find is a single listing, in any provincial early learning register, naming most of what is actually there. The rooms exist in the space between a census that counted them nationally and a registration system that has processed only a fraction of them. To walk this grid at half past seven, past gate after gate opening for the day's first child, is to understand that Ekurhuleni's labour force goes to work on a foundation the state has never finished counting.

### 5. The East Rand Number

**42,000**

**Estimated number of early learning programmes operating across South Africa, per the 2021 national ECD Census — fewer than half fully or conditionally registered with the state**

South Africa's first national Early Childhood Development Census, completed in 2021, counted approximately 42,000 early learning programmes across the country. Of these, only 40% were fully or conditionally registered with the Department of Social Development; a further 16% were still in process, and 42% were unregistered outright. Just 33% received any government subsidy that year. The census counted the rooms. It did not resolve what happens inside most of them, or who pays for it.

### 6. Closing Reflection

**What the Gate Actually Holds**

The woman on the street off Barry Marais Road will open her gate again tomorrow at six, for the reason she has opened it for eleven years: twenty-six families have nowhere else to leave their children, and she has never found it in herself to say no on a morning when the fees fell short. She does not describe this as running a business, though it is one, or as providing infrastructure, though it is that too. She describes it as looking after the children on her street. The question Ekurhuleni has not answered is what it would take for a register built to count classrooms to finally notice the room behind her door.
